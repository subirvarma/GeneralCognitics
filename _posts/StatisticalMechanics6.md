# An Energy based Model for Language and Thinking


## Introduction

This is the third in a series of papers for models of cognition based on the energy minimization principle. 

- The first paper [Generative AI as an Effective Theory of Cognition](https://subirvarma.github.io/GeneralCognitics/2026/07/15/statmech4.html) proposed the Latent Energy based Predictive Processing or LEPP model for perception as a flow process on energy landscapes. This serves as a model for the brain's neo-cortical columns, which are known to act as prediction engines. These can be connected directly to the sensory input-outputs modules, in which case they are predicting what we end up seeing, hearing, smelling etc and their predictions become our perceptions. Alternatively they can be connected to modules that are predicting at a lower level, thus resulting in an hierarchy of predictions that are thought to underlie thinking in the brain. This aspect is explored more fully in this paper.

- The following paper [A Hierarchical Energy-Based Model for Multimodal Cognition](https://subirvarma.github.io/GeneralCognitics/2026/08/07/statmech5.html) built on the LEPP model and proposed the Integrated Multimodal LEPP or IM-LEPP as a more detailed model that integrates perception with language processing. In the brain the integration function happens in the temporal regions that act as integration hubs for signals coming in from the sensory data pipelines, the language and cognition pipeline as well as information from episodic memory stores in the hippocampus and the cortex.

- In this paper we propose a model for episodic memory and its integration with the sensory and language pipelines. We also a propose a model for hierarchical processing in the language pipeline that serves as a model for higher level cognitions functions in the brain. Episodic memory is implemented in the brain with the help of the hippocampus which serves as a 'fast memory' and is able to store episodes after just a single occurrence. Some of these episodes gradually migrate to the 'slow memory' in the cortex over time. During the process of cognition these memories are recalled selectively and they influence the generation of the next thought or word. In addition the design of the energy based prediction module is described in more detail, we propose that it works through a process of energy minimization using multi-step gradient descent. In the process we make connections to the design of Transformers, and show how they are able to mimic the processes taking place in the brain. 

The model for cognition that is proposed here is hierarchical in nature. It has predictive processing pipelines at the lowest level (level 1) that are directly connected to the sensory data coming in through sound or vision. This in turn feeds a higher level pipeline at level 2 that generates the next word in the sequence and in turn influences the perception of the following pieces of sensory data. The word level pipeline is connected to a thought level predictive processing pipeline at level 3 that influences the generation of the next word by conditioning the energy function in the prediction module. The thought level pipeline serves as a scaffold for for word generation, and it in turn can be connected to a higher level predictive processing pipeline operating at level 4. This pipeline has been referred to as a 'schema', and serves as an abstraction that connects higher level cognitive predictions than can multiple areas. 
As an example consider the following example: When writing a book, the word level predictions happen at level 2, while paragraph level predictions are at level 3. The level 4 predictions are at level 4, and we can think of even higher level cognitive pipelines that operate at the level of a series of books on the same topic for example. These higher level cognitive pipelines can also be used to connect ideas from different disciplines which share a higher level schema, for example ideas from mathematics are used to solve problems in physics and vice versa.

The paper proposes models for episodic memory and working memory that are influenced by the ideas of [Baddeley](https://dn790003.ca.archive.org/0/items/MemoryUltimate/Mnemonics/Alan%20Baddeley%20-%20Memory%20(2017).pdf) and Event Segmentation Theory (EST) of [Zacks et.al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2852534/).

- Working memory is defined as the information that is present to our cognition at the current instant in time, i.e., 'what is happening now'. It is multi-modal in nature and incorporates information coming in through the sensory inputs and the state of our cognitive or thought processes. Information that is selectively recalled from episodic memory can also be stored in working memory under the control of the executive controller, and chunks of information of this nature have been referred to as existing in Episodic Buffers by Baddeley.
- The sequence of successive working memory states gets segmented into episodes, and it is these episodes that are stored in the episodic memory. The segmentation is done on the basis of how well the predictions from the predictive processing pipeline at level $n$ agree with the ground-truth from level $n-1$, and if the difference exceeds some threshold, the the event gets segmented. Thus the segmentation into episodes is driven in a bottom-up fashion by the information coming in through the sensory interfaces, as well as in a top-down fashion by the prior information that is stored at higher levels of the cognitive hierarchy as well as the contents of the episodic memory.
- The current episode is always active and serves as a conditioning for the next prediction, so that predictions made across event boundaries sometimes exhibit the 'new room' effect, in which when we step into a new room, we sometimes have difficulty in recalling the thoughts being experienced while in the prior room. Note that segmentation into episodes happens at all levels of the cognitive hierarchy simultaneously.
- The working memory episodes are stored by the hippocampus, which also provides them with a time and place stamp. Thus episodes can be recalled based on time, place or the information in the sequence itself. It has been observed that the event at the segment boundaries receives more extensive processing than the rest of the sequence, which should result in better long-term memory for the boundary event, thus making them easier to access from long term memory. This provides a mechanism for episodic memory recall, by focusing on an associative match of the first event of the episode with the query. 

The IM-LEPP language module that was described in the [previous paper](https://subirvarma.github.io/GeneralCognitics/2026/08/07/statmech5.html) functions as follows: It defines a system latent state, which can be likened to a thought state, and is used to generate language percepts. This state evolves with time as a result of the following events: 

- Modification as a result of new sensory data in the form of phonemes (sound) or characters (vision).
- Modification as a result of energy minimization: The latent state variables can be used to compute an energy function $E_W$ and new sensory data or memory access causes the energy level to rise. It subsequently settles back to a minimum, and this results in a new latent state from which the next word is generated. The energy is a function of the language latent state as well as another state variable that reflects the influence of other sensory modalities (which is identified with the working memory in this paper).

We add the following features to this model in the current paper, resulting in a model we call IM-LEPP2:

- An explicit model for the energy function, and a description of its training and inference process. Energy minimization during inference is done through a variable number of gradient descent steps, the number of steps being a function of the amount of thinking involved in generating the next word.
- A model for hierarchical cognitive processing, in which the output from lower levels serves as ground-truth for the higher levels.
- A model for episodic memory with specifications for segmenting a series of events into episodes, as well as techniques for recalling episodes from memory.
- Specification of the interfaces between the hierarchical cognitive pipelines and the episodic memory.

Since IM-LEPP2 is a new type of language model, how does it compare with other such models that have been proposed over the years? The first language models were based on recurrent neural networks or RNNs, and just like IM-LEPP2, they were also based on a system state that updated over time in a recurrent fashion. However RNNs suffered from the problem that recurrent state lost information over time, hence it did not have a stable long term memory memory. IM-LEPP2 gets around this problem by using an episodic memory storage.

We make deep connections between IM-LEPP2 and Transformer based LLM models. The IM-LEPP1 and IM-LEPP2 models were inspired by models for cognition that have been proposed by cognitive and neuro scientists over the last few decades and the general principle of modeling cognitive processes in terms of energy functions and the process of generation as due to the movement of a state variables on energy landscapes. Since Transformers seem to be doing cognitive tasks that mimic what mimic the brain, does their operation fit within the IM-LEPP2 framework? If so what is the nature of the energy function that it is minimizing, and what are the working memory and episodic memory equivalents? We will show that a type of Transformer called the Loop Transformer shares several features in common with IM-LEPP2, with its context window and KV-cache serving as the equivalent for working memory as well as episodic memory.

Transformers based LLMs solved the memory decay problem in RNNs by not using a recursive design, hence they don't have an explicit system state that gets recursively updated over time. In fact they use the entirety of their initial prefix and past generations as their memory, i.e., their memory is effectively their context window. This has worked very well for them, and with context window sizes of several hundred thousand tokens, it has made possible the powerful models that we see today. However this design does come with a cost since the attention operation has quadratic complexity with respect to the context window size, which has led to the high power requirements for these models. Even though large, the context window is finite, and transformers are not able to access data once it fall outside the window.

Prediction in IM-LEPP2 is based on a multistep minimization of an energy function, in which a context generated by higher level cognitive pipelines, contents of long term memory as well as other sensory modalities serve as a conditioning variable. It can be shown that each Transformer block is roughly equivalent to a single gradient descent step in the minimization of some implicit energy function. This energy function is made explicit in IM-LEPP2 and while the number of blocks in a Transformer column is fixed, IM-LEPP can adjust the number of optimization steps depending upon the difficulty of the prediction.
There are other differences in language generation in the two models that were pointed out in [A Hierarchical Energy-Based Model for Multimodal Cognition](https://subirvarma.github.io/GeneralCognitics/2026/08/07/statmech5.html) that make the IM-LEPP model more biologically plausible. An important difference is whereas the parameters in a Transformer are fixed once its is trained, IM-LEPP can change its parameters during the course of its operations thus leading to continuous learning.

## Organization of Biological Memory

Memory is integral to cognitive functions in the brain, and in this section we will survey a framework that has been proposed on how it is organized, both functionally as well as biologically in the various parts in the brain. In the following sections we will connect this framework with the way memory is implemened memory in IM-LEPP2 and the Transformer.
The description is based on the book [Memory, 2017](https://dn760107.eu.archive.org/0/items/MemoryUltimate/Mnemonics/Alan%20Baddeley%20-%20Memory%20(2017).pdf) by Baddeley, Eysenck and Anderson. 

Human memory can be classified into the following types:

- **Short Term Memory or STM:** This involves simple retention of small amounts of information, tested either immediately or after a short delay. When tested using a sequence of digits, it has been shown that STM capacity is limited to about 6 to 7 digits in most people. Later it was suggested that memory capacity is limited not by the number of items to be recalled, but the number of chunks, where a chunk is defined as a sub-sequence within the larger digit sequence.

  Verbal STM has been modeled using the concept of a phonological loop, which has two sub-components: a short term store and an articulatory rehearsal process. The store is assumed to be limited in capacity and decays within a few seconds, however sound traces can be refreshed by sub-vocal rehearsal (saying the items to yourself). The 6 or 7 limit in STM arises due to the fact that we can say a few digits in sequence before the first digit fades away. Thus this theory posits that STM is based solely on sound, and not on the meaning of the objects being memorized. Long Term Memory or LTM on the other hand is based on semantic content or meaning of the objects being memorized. STM for visual items can also utilize the phonological loop as long as they are nameable.

  STM also exists for visual objects, and has two components, one concerned with spatial memory (where?) and the other concerned with memory for the objects themselves (what?). Spatial location memory shows forgetting over a period of seconds, while object memory is highly accurate to about 4 items, and does not decline. However if we try to remember more than 4 objects, then performance does begin to decline.
Experiments done on patients with brain related impairments have shown that STM and LTM are supported by different regions of the brain, since there have been people in whom one type of memory is impaired while leaving the other type intact.

![](https://subirvarma.github.io/GeneralCognitics/images/stat202.png) 

Figure 1: The Baddeley Working Memory Framework
 
- **Working Memory or WM:** The working memory system not only temporarily stores information, but is also able to manipulate it, thus allowing people to perform complex cognitive functions such as reasoning, learning and comprehension. An example in which working memory would be utilized is when we are adding two multi-digit numbers. This requires us to hold the results of addition of the earlier digits in phonological STM while continuing to add the subsequent digits, with the numbers themselves occupying short term visual memory.

  In order to explain WM Baddeley proposed the framework shown in figure 1. It has several components, starting with the **phonological loop** that holds sequences of acoustic or speech based patterns and is the same as the verbal STM. The **visuo-spatial sketchpad**  performs a similar function for for visually and/or spatially encoded items and arrays. 
  The whole system is controlled by a **Central Executive**, whose job is to focus attention on a limited number of items or tasks at any one time and serves as a controller for the whole system. In the addition example for instance, the two numbers to be added would be in visual-spatial sketchpad, while the results of the addition would end up in the phonological loop, while the Central Executive decides which two digits to focus on and also does the actual addition.
  
  Under the control of the Central Executive, we are able to mentally manipulate objects that are in the visuo-spatial sketchpad, for example mentally rotate an object. As in the addition example, the phonological loop and the visuo-spatial sketchpad often work together to enhance performance. Since the Central Executive can only focus on a few items at a time, sometimes the information coming through the senses can interfere with the information in the phonological loop and the visuo-spatial sketchpad and vice-versa.
  
  Baddeley also added Long Term Memory or LTM to the WM framework (we will also refer to it as Episodic Memory in this paper). The LTM was added to take into account the fact that sometimes our attention is focussed on objects or sounds that are not located in the phonological loop or the visuo-spatial sketchpad, and hence must have been retrieved from LTM. In order to interface the LTM with the Central Executive, he introduced the  the **Episodic Buffer** or EB, which he assumed is a storage system that can hold about four chunks of information in amodal code. As a result of the multi-modality, he hypothesized that the EB also holds information from the phonological loop and the visuo-spatial sketchpad, and thus acts as a link between the various sub-systems as shown in figure 1. According to the model, information is retrieved from the episodic buffer through conscious awareness, thus linking the working memory model with [Baar's theory of consciousness](https://ccrg.cs.memphis.edu/assets/papers/1988/Baars-A%20Cognitive%20Theory%20of%20Consciousness.pdf) (a shorter description can be found [here](https://tilde.ini.uzh.ch/~kiper/Baars_1.pdf)). Thus working memory in the Baddeley framework is identical to the Global Workspace proposed by Baar as a model for consciousness since in both cases conscious awareness serves the function of pulling together separate streams of information from various senses (and memory) and binding them into perceived objects and scenes.
Recent evidence suggests that the Episodic Buffer functions as "display" system for infomation coming in from the various sources, however active manipulation of the objects in the Episodic Buffer is done elsewhere, in the blocks labeled as semantics in figure 1.

  The amodal contents of the EB gets periodically gets aggregated into larger chunks or episodes and stored into the LTM, and these constitute our memory. The process of chunking has been clarified in recent years and is described below. During the process of memory recall, one or more episodes from the LTM are chosen and these are fed into the audio-visual generation systems and then to the visuo-spatial sketchpad (and the phonological loop) to generate our 'inner eye', i.e., our ability to imagine events and sounds from our past. Note that the process of recall is under the control of the Central Executive, so that we are not constantly reminiscing about the past. Ultimately the recalled memory makes its way into the EB, so that at any one time, the EB has information about what is happening currently in real time, as well as a few representations for information that has been recalled from the LTM.

  I have added a couple of arrows(shown in red) to Baddeley's diagram, which shows the broadcast of the real time information in EB (vision and sound) to the generation modules, which then use this information to predict the next image (or sound). This so called flooding of information from a central aggregation module to the rest of the brain is integral to Baar's theory of consciousness.
 
- **Episodic Memory:** The concept of episodic memory was introduced by [Endel Tulving](https://turing.iimas.unam.mx/~luis/cursos/MARE2025-2/lecturas/Tulving.pdf) in 1972 to emphasize the difference between the recollection of specific events stamped by time and location, vs semantic memory which is generalized knowledge of the world. Episodic memory enables "mental time travel", allowing us to relive past episodes as well "travel forward" and anticipate future events. It is thought that episodic memory is accumulated and consolidated to form the basis for semantic memory, so that disruption to the episodic memory system also leads to inability to form semantic memory. Episodic memory is also called fast memory since it requires just a single episode to store information, while semantic memory storage happens over a longer time scale. Encoding and retrieval of episodic memory is easier if the material is meaningful and well organized, i.e., episodic memory is encoded at a higher semantic level compared to sound or vision traces that we encountered for STM. When trying to store a word in episodic memory, it helps if there is an image associated with the word, so that the word can be recalled both visually and verbally.

  There is a special category of episodic memory called autobiographical memory, and this refers to memories that we hold regarding ourselves and our relations to the world around us. Some aspects of autobiographical memory are semantic in nature, such as facts about ourselves (our phone number for example). Autobiographical memory has been investigated using diary studies, and it has been found that the recall level decreases as time passes, but this also depends on the nature of recall cues provided, with who, what and where cues providing better recall then when cues. Also in general more frequent recall helps to retain the item in memory. Within this category, the concept of flashbulb memory has been proposed to capture the case when some event is recalled with almost photographic detail provided extreme emotion coincided with its actual occurrence.

- **Semantic Memory:** This is defined as general knowledge of the world and is not tied down to a specific time or place. The pre frontal cortex or PFC has been shown to be involved in semantic memory, while episodic memory is associated with the hippocampus. Semantic memory is organized in the brain on the basis of semantic relatedness or semantic distance, i.e., how closely related pairs of words are. In machine learning terms, this implies that the brain stores latent representations.
  
  The different properties of an object are stored in different areas in the brain. For example when we recall a seagull for example, the visual area gets activated with an image of the bird while the sound areas are activated with its call.
Semantic memory for objects that can be visualized is stored differently and in a different part of the neo-cortex  according to the sensory-functional theory which says that information about objects in semantic memory is organized in terms of distinction between sensory properties (visual, auditory, taste, tactile) and functional properties or non-perceptual properties (what an object does or what is used for). Using a process which has not been explained yet by traditional neuroscience, the brain manages to integrate all the different types of information associated with an object rapidly and automatically when we think about any given concept (with the IM-LEPP2 model we present a theory for how this is done).

  In addition to object level information, semantic memory is also thought to store knowledge of schemas and one such type of sechema is called a script. For example the sequence of events that happens when we go out to eat at a restaurant (entering, giving reservation name, ordering drinks, discussing the menu etc) form a schema. Schemas help us in forming expectation, and if they are violated then we take action.

  Patients suffering from semantic dementia have severe problems accessing the meaning of words but often retain access to script knowledge, which implies that semantic and script information is stored in different parts of the brain, with the former being associated with the anterior temporal region of the brain and the latter with the prefrontal cortex.

- **Memory Recall:** Memories are recalled by means of cues or queries. In the case of episodic memory, these can be in the form of time or place for the event, as well as various retrieval cues that were associated with the event at the time of occurrence (also called keys in machine learning terms). Sometimes the memory is present but we are not able to access it due to lack of appropriate cues to activate it. In general, the more cues associated with a memory, the easier it is to recall it. Any aspect of of the content of memory can serve as a reminder that could access the experience, this is known as content addressability. The power of a cue depends upon the attention given to it during the retrieval process.

  Memories have activation levels, which determines how accessible an episode is in memory.  An episode's activation level increases when we perceive something associated with it, or when we think of it directly. The activation persists for some time even after the attention has been removed. If there are multiple cues present, then the activation levels for particular episodes additively increases in proportion to the individual cues. For a cue to be useful, it needs to be present at the encoding, and encoded with the desired episode. Memories with higher activation are easier to recall.

  We are not constantly bombarded by memories, we have to be in the right frame of mind to recollect the past (mediated by the controller?). Pg 171 We are aware of episodic memories while they are being recalled. However there is also the case that memories can influence us without an associated recall, so that we are unaware of the connection, this is called implicit memory. In terms of the framework in figure 1, this implies a direct connection between the LTM module and the EB, thus bypassing the sensory modules all together.

- **Segmentation of Event Sequences into Episodes** Even though the information coming in through the senses happens in a continuous stream, our memory is organized in the form of distinct episodes which are chunks of subjective experience that have a beginning and an end. The brain uses the episode that is currently happening as the principal context while events from the recent past are rapidly forgotten which unburdens the mind  from potentially obsolete working memory representations. Context shifts from an episode to the next can be caused by changes in narrative (while reading or listening to word), spatial (usually caused due to motion, for example when we step into a new room) or other perceptual shifts. If there are items that appear on both sides of an episode boundary, their sequential binding is reduced, thus leading to the phenomenon of people forgetting what they came for when they step into a new room.

  [Zacks et.al, 2007](https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/sites.wustl.edu/dist/e/952/files/2017/09/zackspsychbull07-sbm4za.pdf) proposed a theory for segmentation which they called Event Segmentation Theory or EST (episodes are called events in their paper). It states that episode boundaries are created when the sensory pipelines prediction differs significantly from the ground truth evidence coming the sensory inputs. They also proposed the idea of an **Event Model** whose output is used to guide the prediction module at the sensory level and this output stays constant during a single episode, thus guiding all the sensory predictions with a stable baseline. The change in the event model output at the episode boundary clears the old context, thus leading to effect that was mentioned earlier of abrupt change in our cognition when moving to a new room. They also proposed that the predictions of the event model are governed by a still igher level predictive model
which they called a **event schemata** in their paper. Event schemata contain previously learned information about the sequential structure of the event model.
At the times when the sensory level predictions differ significantly from the input data, a new episode is initiated and at the same time the event model gets updated.
In general there can be several levels in this hierarchy, with the output of the event schemata controlled by its own model operating at higher level, which in turn gets updated with the information coming in through the event sequence. The sequence at all these levels can be segmented into episodes with the segmentation becomes progressively finer as we proceed from higher levels to lower levels.
Zacks et.al. also proposed that the perceptual information from event boundaries is preferentially accessible in LTM, thus making them an attractive target for the associative recall process.

## The IM-LEPP Model

![](https://subirvarma.github.io/GeneralCognitics/images/stat170.png) 

Figure 1: The IM-LEPP Model

The IM-LEPP model is shown in figure 1. It proposes a hierarchical spatial integration structure for the vision model, it introduces a hierarchical temporal integration structure for language, and finally it proposes how the two may be integrated together to create a common representation. The IM-LEPP model has the following features:

- There is a central ATL type hub at level 1 that integrates representations coming in from the vision and language hubs. Note that the communication between the central hub and the vision and language hubs is bi-directional, so that not only do the spoke hubs influence the representation in the central hub, but they in turn are influenced by the information coming from the central hub.
- The vision hub itself has a two level structure. The central vision hub at level 2 integrates information coming from several simultaneously active level 3 predictive processing pipelines. There is a level 3 pipeline for each of the objects in the scene, as well an always-on pipeline for the scene itself, and all these get integrated at the level 2 vision hub. The representation of each of these pipelines evolves asynchronously in time and the level 2 hub integrates the latest information from each individual object and sends it up to the central level 1 hub. Note that the per object pipelines come and go depending on which objects are currently in the field of vision, while the scene level pipeline is always active.
- The per-object level 3 predictive processing pipelines operate according to the inference-prediction-generation framework that was used for the LEPP model. The system state that results from the inference module in this pipeline is sent to the level 2 vision hub for integration with the states of all the other objects in the scene. This combined representation in turn gets integrated with representations from other modalities in the multimodal level 1 hub. The integrated level 1 representation in turn is fed back to the prediction module in the level 3 object predictive processing pipelines, and the result is used to generate the next percept. Note that these percepts take all the other objects that are in the scene into account (as well as other modalities), by virtue of this architecture.
- The language sub-system also has a two level hierarchical structure, however the hierarchy is in time rather than in space. At the lower level of the hierarchy at level 3 are predictive processing pipelines that operate at the discrete phoneme level (in the case of spoken language) or at the character level (in the case of reading). This level incorporates an inference-prediction-generation modules whose job is to predict the next phoneme or character. Note that unlike the case for vision, only one of the level 3 pipelines is active at any one time; moreover, within whichever pipeline is active, phonemes or characters necessarily arrive one at a time in sequence rather than simultaneously, unlike the multiple objects that can be concurrently present in a visual scene. Together these account for the temporal, rather than spatial, character of the language hub.
The latent representation from this level is sampled at certain discrete instants that contain representations for whole words, and these are fed as input into a word level predictive processing pipeline at level 2. The next word latent prediction done at this level is influenced by the state of the central ATL hub and thus gets modified by information from the other modalities, and ultimately gets sent to the level 3 hub to generate percepts. If the phoneme hub is active then it generates percepts in the form of sound or if the character hub is active then it generates percepts in the form of written text.

Thus the IM-LEPP model paints a picture in which there are number of distributed, predictive processing pipeline modules in the brain, that are individually responsible for predictions in the modality they are tracking. Hence the prediction operations happens in a distributed manner, while central hubs at level 1 and level 2 are responsible for integrating the lower level representations, and in turn feeding them back to the predictive processing pipelines.
All state changes in this model at the various pipelines and hubs are based on the principle of the energy minimization, and thus provide a plausible model for the brain's operation at Marr's level 2.

The [previous paper](https://subirvarma.github.io/GeneralCognitics/2026/08/07/statmech5.html) left several aspects of this model un-specified, in particular details about the operation of the memory module and its interface with the other modules, as well as details of how the energy function $E_W$ is implemented in the prediction module. We will focus on these aspects of the design in this paper. 

The IM-LEPP language model consists of a lower level phoneme or character level model and a higher word level model. In the following we first describe the predictive processing pipeline for characters, followed by that for words.

![](https://subirvarma.github.io/GeneralCognitics/images/stat181.png) 

Figure 2: Predictive Processing Pipeline for Next Character Prediction

Consider the simple case of child reading, in which case he or she reads one character at a time. Adults on the other hand use a faster system in which multiple characters are read at time in order to speed up the process, which comes after practice. We will assume that the image of an individual character coming in from the visual system is sent to a classifier, that outputs a discrete number indicating the category. There can be 26-70 different categories depending upon the punctuation. This process results in a discrete sequence $(ch_1,...,ch_N)$ that is fed into a predictive processing pipeline.

The discrete character sequence is sent into a predictive processing pipeline shown in figure 2 that creates an latent representation $z_n$ for each character using the predictive coding function $q_{\phi}$, followed by prediction of the next latent representation $x_{n+1}$ using a energy based model $E_{CH}(x;z_n,u_{n+1}=yy_m)$. 
Note that this prediction is conditioned on not just the previous character level latent $z_n$, but also on a latent representation $yy_m$ for the $m^{th}$ word (whose characters are being generated), which comes from the level 2 word pipeline. The computation of $yy_m$ is explained below when we discuss the word level pipeline.
Next $x_{n+1}$ is converted into a character using the predictive coding function $g_{\psi}$. The generated character $y_{n+1}=g_{\psi}(x_{n+1})$ is compared with the actual next character $ch_{n+1}$, and the error generated by this information is used to estimate the next representation $z_{n+1}$. These operations are illustrated in the above figure.

The system is able to detect the end of a word by tracking the difference between the predicted  latent state $x_{n+1}$ and the actual latent state $z_{n+1}$. 
If this difference exceeds some threshold, then the model assumes that the previous character $ch_n$ occurred at the end of a word (saw the $m^{th}$ word $w_m$), and the representation $z_n$ is sent over to the word level pipeline as discussed next. We also use the empty space at the end of each word to infer the word ending, however there some languages such as Chinese and Old Latin that don't use spaces (see [Saenger 1997](https://www.sup.org/books/literary-studies-and-literature/space-between-words)), and the alternative mechanism described here is more general, and also works for the case of auditory signals.

The predictive coding pipeline at the word level uses the latent state $z_n$ from the character pipeline as the ground truth that represents the latent representation for for the $m^{th}$ word. Note that the word level subscript $m$ for the current word  is not the same as the character level subscript $n$ for obvious reasons.
This pipeline creates a higher level latent word representation $zz_m$ by modifying the existing representation $xx_m$ to $q_{\phi}(xx_m,z_n)$.
$zz_m$ is then sent to the level 1 central ATL hub where it gets modified by the vision data to the latent $zzz'_m$. For example if the current image is that of an apple, then this is reflected in $zzz'_m$. The latent variable is then fed into the episodic memory store, where it is used to match with memory elements using a process that is explained below. This results in the retrieval of one or more memory elemets that modify the latent $zzz'_m$ to $zzz_m$, so that now this latent has information from both other sensory modalities, as well as the memory store.

The latent $zzz_m$ is then fed back to the level 2 word pipeline where it is used to predict the next word by using the
the energy based prediction module $E_W(x;zz_m,zzz_m)$ and this results in the prediction $xx_{m+1}$ for the next word latent (see above figure).
Note that, unlike the vision hub at level 2, which performs integration only, the word-level pipeline includes its own dedicated prediction step. This reflects the fact that phoneme-level prediction serves segmentation, while word-level prediction serves ordinary sentence-level anticipation, a genuinely distinct function operating at a different timescale. This mechanism also illustrates why the IM-LEPP model may be able to learn new words faster, since the predicted word is not only a function of the previous word level context $zz_m$, but is also influenced by the vision and modalities by means of the latent $zzz_m$. 
Similarly the emotional related information coming in through the valence system (valence) can influence our choice of the next word.

$xx_{m+1}$ is subsequently used to generate the next word latent $yy_{m+1}=g_{\psi}(xx_{m+1})$, and this value is fed back into the  level 3 character level predictive processing pipeline shown in figure 10, where it influences the prediction of the next character $x_{n+2}$ through the energy function $E_{CH}(x;z_{n+1},yy_{m+1})$. This in turn gets modified by the other characters in the next word being read. When the last character of that word is encountered, then the latent representation at that time $z_{n+k}$ (where $k$ is the number of characters in the word just read) is fed back into the word level model to correct the prediction $yy_{m+1}$, and this closes the word level prediction loop. As pointed out in the introduction, this is also an hierarchical system, but the hierarchy is in time rather than in space, as was the case for the visual system.

For the case when we are doing character generation, i.e., writing, this feedback loop between the character level and word level pipelines is still active. In this case it serves as a verification of whether the word that was generated at level 3 matches the word that the level 2 word level system meant to generate.

Do the latent states $zzz_m$ or $zz_m$ encode 'thought'? 
Levelt’s production model as described in his book [Speaking: From Intention to Articulation (1989)](https://www.mpi.nl/publications/item67053/speaking-intention-articulation) has a first stage, conceptualization, whose output is a pre-verbal message i.e., a language-independent conceptual representation of what to say, prior to and dissociable from any particular verbalization (which is why “the same thought” can be expressed in different words or languages). This is a direct architectural instantiation of exactly the proposed role for $zzz_m$ as a persistent, amodal state that the generative pathway then unrolls into a word sequence, with $zzz_m$ playing the role of the preverbal message and the language pipeline playing Levelt’s formulation stage.

The proposed system models the process of thinking or reasoning at two levels: (1) Through the multistage process by which the energy $E_W$ settles to a minimum value (this is explained below), and (2) Through multiple invocations of the prediction module, each time with a new conditioning vector $zzz_m$. This process happens entirely in latent space, in which the representation $zz_m$ changes over time in response to both external and internal influences. 

This model does not like the Transformer model at first glance, but as explained in the following sections, it has the two main components of the Transformer within it, with the integration of episodic memory corresponding to the attention operation and each stage of the energy $E_W$ minimization corresponding to the operation of the feed forward network or FFN in Transformers. Hence this model can be likened to a Universal Transformer with a variable number of attention+FFN blocks in some sense. However unlike Transformers, the system uses a recurrent state $zz_n$, which makes it a fundamentally different design, and closer to biological systems.

Figure 4: Energy minimization steps in the prediction module

We will adopt the methodology of directly minimizing the energy function through multiple gradient descent steps, as shown in the above figure. In the previous papers we described an alternative probabilistic algorithm for doing this, using several stages of noise annealing, with multiple Langevin diffusion steps at each stage. This algorithm requires us to use a preset number of annealing stages, since the noise series has to follow a pre-defined schedule. This works well for image prediction systems, however in the case of language it runs into the problem that in the case of reasoning models, the number of stages is a function of the level of difficulty of the problem being solved and hence cannot be set in advance. Working directly with the energy function avoids this problem since we can vary the number of stages as a function of a stopping rule.
This design also has the benefit that the parameters for $E_W$ can be learnt by focusing on the final stage, thus avoiding the need to back propagate to the earlier stages. This algorithm is described in detail later in the paper.

The incorporation of memory into the system is a critical aspect of the design. Human memory is of the following types:

- Working memory: This is defined as the information that is available to us at any instant of time through the data coming in though our senses. The sensory data, such as vision or sound, lingers within the brain for a short instant before disappearing, and this allows us to chain together discrete images into a moving picture for example. The same is true for sound, and enables us to remember the last few phonemes and thus put together a word by connecting them.
- Episodic memory or Fast Memory: This is memory for a sequence of events that play out over time and at a particular place.  The hippocampus plays a critical role in saving episodic memory sequences, and giving them a time stamp and place stamp. Given the right conditions, even a single sequence of events gets stored in episodic memory, thus accounting for the fast memory moniker.

## Transformer Models

In the last few years there have been several proposals that have been made to improve upon Transformer models. These proposals can be broadly classified into the following categories:

- Loop Transformers and Universal Transformers: The basic idea in these models is to repeat a single Transformer block multiple times, and has been shown to have similar performance to a full Transformer, while saving on parametric memory. This aspect is directly incorporated in IM-LEPP, since if we regard the $E_W$ energy module as being roughly equivalent to a Transformer block, then multiple invocations of the energy module during the process of energy minimization are equivalent to the multiple invocations of the Loop Transformer block, with the caveat that all invocations in either case use the same set of module parameters.
- Techniques to propagate the latent state: The amount of thinking that a Transformer can do is tied to the number of blocks it has in each column, which is a fixed number. In some sense the thinking process has to re-start in a new prediction column, and the information in the latent state from the previous column is lost. There have been some recent proposals to correct this by propagating the final latent state from between successive columns, most notably in the Full Bandwidth Transformer. This is done natively in the IM-LEPP design, since the system latent state is propagated between successive prediction stages.
- Techniques to break up a long context into chunks, and then choose a subset of these chunks during the prediction process: IM-LEPP incorporates this design choosing appropriate chunks from its episodic memory store during the prediction process.

In summary, recent Transformer models have begun to incorporate features that make them closer to the IM-LEPP2 design. Since IM-LEPP2 itself was inspired by experimental evidence collected over the years from neuroscience about how the brain operates, this convergence brings the latest Transformer design improvements closer to the biological brain.
There is another class of models that have been proposed recently called reasoning models.
In Transformers reasoning requires multiple invocations of the prediction block within a column, and if the number of blocks is fixed, then it leads limitations in the reasoning process. Some information is propagated to the next column using the predicted token, but most of the information in the final latent state is lost. The IM-LEPP model facilitates reasoning through two mechanisms: 
(1) Through the multistep gradient descent based minimzation of the energy function, in which the number of steps is variable, hence can change depending upon the difficulty of the reasoning problem, 
(2) Since the final latent state is sent to the next prediction module, this avoids the problem that Transformers have of loosing the latent state information. Furthermore the new prediction module utilizes fresh conditioning vectors, which prevent the recurrent state from decaying over time.
